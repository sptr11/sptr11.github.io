Use case 3 · → a person's agent to a company

Your agent and a company

A person's assistant phones a business, shops a store, or signs in to an account. The company decides how far to let it in, and today its only choices are all or nothing.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The short answer

A person's agent deals with a company's staff, website, or own agent. It travels by phone, browser, and sign-in page. The company sets the limits, either by blocking the agent or by letting it act as the person. A draft protocol proposes the middle ground, and no company has adopted it yet.

Who is on each end

An assistant calling a nail salon

The first consumer agent to reach businesses at volume used the oldest channel there is. Google's Ask for Me feature phones local businesses, beginning with auto repair shops and nail salons, to ask about prices and availability, and reports back. Each call opens by announcing an automated system calling from Google on a user's behalf, and businesses can opt out through their Google Business Profile (CX Today). The service has since rolled out to users nationwide (CO/AI).

On the web, the person's agent is a browser that clicks for them. When Perplexity's Comet browser shopped on Amazon, Amazon objected and sued. A court blocked the bot in March 2026 (GeekWire), and an appeals court sided with Perplexity in August (Northeast Times). Nobody had set any limits in advance, so a court had to.

Sometimes the company answers with an agent of its own. Customer-support agents from Sierra, Decagon, and others now sit behind many help pages, so a person's assistant may be talking to a company's assistant without either party saying so. The company's agent is usually built to talk to a person, which is the case the law expects.

What carries the message

The phone, the browser, and the sign-in page

None of these exchanges uses an agent protocol. They ride the channels a person would use, which is why they work today and why the company can't tell a careful assistant from a scraper. At work, the channel is different. An employee's agent reaching a business application can go through the employer's identity provider using Okta's Cross App Access, an OAuth extension called ID-JAG that is now a stable part of MCP (MCP).

That path covers less than it seems. Workers at more than 90% of companies use personal AI tools for work, while only 40% of companies buy an official subscription (MIT NANDA via Virtualization Review). A large share of work-related agent traffic therefore runs on consumer accounts outside any company directory, where enterprise sign-in never sees it.

Who sets the limits, and where a person approves

All or nothing, and a proposed ladder

A company facing someone's agent has two choices today. It can block the agent, or it can let the agent sign in as the person and do anything the person can. The draft Personal Agent Protocol, known as Poppy, starts from that observation and proposes a ladder between the two (Personal Agent Protocol, draft 0.2).

On the first rung, any agent may search. On the second, an agent may see the person's account after the person signs in on the company's own page. On the third, it may act, with the person's approval. A company publishes what it supports at /.well-known/poppy.json, offers whichever interfaces it has (APIs, MCP, web pages, or its own agent), and keeps one session across all of them. Both sides of a conversation flag whether a person or an AI is speaking. The draft is Apache-licensed, names no steward, and lists no adopters.

Poppy settles the identity question in one direction. The agent belongs to the person, and the company decides what it may do once the person signs in. That works because the person already has an account there. It does not cover two companies, where the person on one side has no account with the other.

What's missing

Rules that protect the person, but only the person

This is the only cell of the grid that the law addresses, and every rule protects a natural person on the receiving end. Article 50 of the EU AI Act has required disclosure of AI interaction since 2 August 2026, and its final code of practice on labelling does not mention agents talking to agents (Jones Day). The FCC treats AI voices as "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, so outbound AI calls need prior consent (Cooley). Maine and Utah have chatbot disclosure laws (Wiley), and Colorado repealed its general disclosure duty in May 2026 (McDermott).

Comfort is moving the other way from supply. Trust in fully autonomous agents fell from 43% to 27% in a year (Capgemini, Jul 2025), and only 20% of surveyed business leaders trust agents to make financial transactions on their own (PwC). People want their assistants to do more, and they want to see what is done before it is final.

The missing piece is the business version of Poppy's ladder: graduated permissions between two organizations, tied to a contract rather than a personal account. Microsoft states that Entra agent identities "can't access resources or APIs in other tenants" (Microsoft Learn), and NIST left external agents out of its first project on agent identity (NCCoE).
