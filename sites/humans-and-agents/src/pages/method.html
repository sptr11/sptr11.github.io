Method

The framework, the evidence, eight testable claims and where they stand, and the limits of a desk study written in a field that changes monthly.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The framework

The guide classifies every exchange on two axes. The mode is who composes and who sends on each side. Person to person is the baseline of email, chat, and phone. Person and agent covers a person dealing with their own agent or with someone else's; in the second case the person may not know they are talking to software. Agent to agent means no human in the turn, with agents acting under rules fixed in advance. Both sides drafting means each side is a principal working through an agent: the agents read and draft, and the people decide and send.

The scope is how many trust domains the exchange crosses. Direct exchanges are between individuals. Within a company means one identity provider, one policy, and one legal entity, so identity is solved and policy is internal. Across companies means two trust domains with no shared identity provider and, at most, a contract between them. Every trust question gets harder there: who is this, may they see this, can they bind their company, and whose record wins a dispute.

The dividing line throughout is whether a party can bind its principal. That is a restatement of commitment theory from the multi-agent literature (Singh 1999), and it is why the guide treats signed mandates as the decisive artifact.

The evidence

The work began as a research brief and was then checked figure by figure. Twenty-seven figures and protocol statuses were re-verified against the measuring organization's own page, the specification text, the RFC Editor, the IETF Datatracker, or an SEC filing. In all, 10 held as written, seven were corrected, seven were qualified, and three could not be confirmed. The largest correction was agent payment volume: a widely repeated $24M in 30 days became about $1.6M once wash trading was removed.

Three instruments carry most of the argument. A catalog of 25 public cross-company deployments, coded by mode, who sets the limits, and where a person approves, appears in part on the use-case pages. A six-property rubric scores 13 standards on delegation and consent, from specification text, on the Protocols page. And a systematic literature review covers 70 core sources, each checked against a primary metadata record (arXiv, Crossref, the RFC Editor, or the venue).

The quality profile is a finding in itself. The strata closest to the present are mostly unrefereed: 8 of 12 modern protocol surveys and six of nine agent-market studies are preprints written within months of the protocols they describe. Claims about current agent capability therefore lean on the peer-reviewed anchors, including the most-cited survey of language-model agents (Wang et al., Frontiers of Computer Science 2024), CAMEL (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023), and Generative Agents (Park et al., UIST 2023).

The modern surveys map transport and leave out consent. They compare MCP, IBM's ACP, A2A, and ANP (Ehtesham et al.), separate context-oriented from inter-agent protocols (Yang et al.), and split communication into user-agent, agent-agent, and agent-environment classes that parallel this guide's modes (Kong et al.). None treats policy, consent, or audit between organizations as a layer. Identity research authenticates the agent rather than the cross-company principal: Chan et al.'s agenda of attribution, interaction-shaping, and remediation (TMLR 2025) maps onto identity, consent, and audit, and South et al. extend OAuth so a person can delegate to an agent, within one identity provider (2025). No academic proposal specifies how one company's identity provider vouches for a named employee to another company's agent.

Four gaps in the literature remain. No protocol survey treats consent between organizations. No study observes a live cross-company agent market with real money; all the academic evidence comes from simulation. No incident data ranks agent attack classes by how attacks start. And no controlled study places agents from separate principals across a real trust boundary.

Eight hypotheses and where they stand

Each claim was written so that evidence could prove it wrong. Supported means the evidence leans toward it and no falsifier has been met. Needs rewording means the evidence splits, or the test counts the wrong thing. Untestable yet means the data the test needs does not exist.

H1 and H5 hold, and both need precision rather than reversal. Suppliers accept Walmart's limits rather than negotiate them, so "agreed in advance" overstates how the rules are made. And Okta's Cross App Access already has one company's identity provider vouching for a person to another company's app, but that person already has an account at the second company.

H7 is the claim most at risk. Phishing's ranking rests on decades of standardized reporting of how breaches start, and agent incidents have no equivalent. In one 2026 survey, 53% of security leaders admitted withholding breach reports (HiddenLayer), and vendors often classify injection-driven data flows as intended behavior, which keeps them out of vulnerability counts.

What remains to be measured

Desk evidence now bears on every research question, and four pieces of primary work remain. A quarterly crawl of the Tranco top million domains for agent manifests would be the first of its kind; the only one found ran a 10-host trial in October 2026. A scripted matrix of eight assistants against six hosting setups and several edge defaults would isolate why assistants fail to reach a page. A controlled red-team experiment would compare raw A2A, a deterministic policy gateway, and human-approved sending against one set of attacks, including a manipulation arm with no injection at all. And 25 interviews with IT administrators, security and legal reviewers, support leaders, and freight operators would measure what no survey does: how often agents already act toward other companies, and what approval thresholds firms use.

Two further measurements follow from the hypotheses. Testing H2 needs a consented sample of business email run through an AI-text detector and compared with the same firms' agent logs. Testing H7 needs an incident dataset with a primary attack vector coded by two people under a written rule.

Limits of this work

Source quality. Several figures rest on company or second-hand sources and are marked as such where they appear: Pactum's spend under management, ACP's checkout change, UCP's partners, several acquisition prices, and freight operating data from Parade and C.H. Robinson, which disclose no method.

Speed. A2A's steward and Web Bot Auth's standing at the IETF both changed within eight weeks of the first draft. Every figure carries a date and should be read as of 18 October 2026.

Subjective coding. The maturity grid and the standards scorecard reflect one coder's judgment. A full study should re-score both with two independent coders and report inter-rater agreement.

Visibility. Public deployments over-represent vendor marketing, and private agent use between companies is invisible to the catalog. Incident counts undercount too.

Conflict of interest. The author builds products in this space. The hypotheses should be re-registered before primary data is collected, and the red-team experiment replicated by someone without a stake.

Analogy. Email, chat, and EDI suggest patterns; they don't predict. On the web, the winner bundled trust with delivery, which blurs any clean split between the two. And agents differ from every earlier receiver in one way: they can be persuaded by the message itself.
