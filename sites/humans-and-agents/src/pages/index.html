A field guide · October 2026

How humans and agents talk

Software agents now send messages for people. Almost none of those messages reach another company's agent. This guide covers where they go instead, what carries them, and what is still missing.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026 · Five use cases, five background chapters, 221 sources

For three decades the internet carried messages between two kinds of endpoint, people and servers. Between 2024 and 2026 a third arrived. A delegated agent is software that acts for a named person, reads and writes ordinary language, and can do things with consequences. It books, pays, negotiates, and writes email.

This guide asks a narrow question about that new endpoint. When an agent's message crosses from one company to another, who is on the other end, and who can prove what was approved? The answer, as of October 2026, is that a person usually is, and that almost nobody can prove it.

What the evidence shows

Agents cross company lines by talking to people. Of 25 public cross-organization deployments catalogued for this study, two put language-model agents on both sides. One is a non-binding data-sharing pilot between Tyson Foods and Gordon Food Service. The other is a contract-negotiation demo from 2023. Everything with real volume follows one of three patterns: an agent dealing with the other firm's people, an agent paying or tendering against a posted price, or agents drafting while people send.

One side always sets the limits. Every autonomous exchange runs inside limits fixed in advance, and anything outside them goes to a person. Walmart sets the ceiling its negotiation bot works within, and the supplier's representative accepts or declines. None of the 25 deployments shows two companies' agents disclosing and reconciling their limits with each other.

Trust is being built in email's order, only faster, and from the receiving end. Email waited 24 to 33 years for sender authentication. Agents reached the same stage in about two. As with email, the receivers moved first: Cloudflare, Akamai, and AWS verify agent signatures in production, while the IETF's core document has been a working-group draft only since September 2026.

Consent between strangers exists in one place, and it is payments. Only AP2 and Mastercard's Verifiable Intent record, in a form a stranger can check, what a named person authorized. They work because a card issuer vouches for the person. No standard can withdraw a long-lived agent delegation before it expires. Acquirers have paid about $41B for identity and data-security companies on the strength of machine and agent identities, yet no company reports material revenue from agents.

Five use cases

Each use case is answered with the same four questions, in the same order: who is on each end, what carries the message, who sets the limits and where a person approves, and what is missing.

  1. 1

    Buying and negotiating

    A buyer's agent bargains with a supplier's person. The buyer writes every limit in advance.

    → agent to person, across companies
  2. 2

    Paying

    Agents pay against posted prices and spending caps. Payments hold the only consent record a stranger can verify.

    → agent to priced endpoint
  3. 3

    Your agent and a company

    A person's assistant phones a business, shops a store, or signs in to an account. The company decides how far to let it in.

    → person's agent to a company
  4. 4

    Reading a page for someone

    Assistants fetch pages for people. The network edge now decides which of them get through.

    → assistant to website
  5. 5

    Correspondence between companies

    Agents draft and people send, mostly by email. Nobody measures how much, and the attacks arrive the same way.

    ↔ both sides drafting
