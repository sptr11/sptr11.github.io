Paying

Agents already pay for things. They pay machines that post a price, or merchants under caps a person set. Payments are also the only place where an approval between strangers leaves a record anyone can check.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The short answer

An agent pays a paid API or a merchant. The message is an HTTP request or a card transaction. The person who funds the wallet or holds the card sets the limits, sometimes approving each purchase with a passkey and sometimes not at all. What's missing is any version of this consent record for actions that aren't payments.

Who is on each end

A wallet on one side, a price list on the other

The busiest agent-to-agent traffic between organizations is not a negotiation. It is a machine paying a machine. Under x402, a server answers a request with HTTP status 402, Payment Required, and names a price, and the agent pays it in a stablecoin. On the Base network, x402 has carried more than 138M transactions, 87% of them under 10 cents (Chainalysis via AI Weekly).

The card networks are building the merchant version. Visa Intelligent Commerce is live in Europe with more than 30 issuers, and the cardholder confirms each purchase with a passkey (Visa). Mastercard and Santander completed a live agent payment in Europe in which the account holder had set caps and merchant categories beforehand and approved nothing at the moment of sale (ai2.work). That was one transaction.

Shopping assistants sit in between. Google's Universal Commerce Protocol, built with Shopify, powers checkout inside AI Mode and Gemini and is expanding to Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom (Google). OpenAI and Stripe's Agentic Commerce Protocol continues, though ChatGPT scaled its native checkout back to product discovery in March 2026 (Digital Commerce 360, secondary).

What carries the message

Six protocols and very little money

At least six payment protocols are in market: AP2, ACP, UCP, x402, Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol, and Mastercard Agent Pay. Only x402 publishes data, and the data is sobering. TRM Labs counts about $52.7M settled over x402 since May 2025, of which about $25.6M looks like commerce and only 0.6% to 7.5% of that looks agentic. It puts actual agent payment activity in 2026 at roughly $5,000 to $11,000 a month (TRM Labs).

Andreessen Horowitz reached a similar answer from the other direction. A headline of $24M in 30 days shrinks to about $1.6M once wash trading is removed, and most of what remains is developer tools paying per call from wallets that people fund (a16z via Cointelegraph, secondary). The card-rail protocols publish no volume at all.

The money that has moved is in the rails, not the agents. Stripe bought the stablecoin company Bridge for $1.1B, and Tempo raised $500M at a $5B valuation. Both bets are about stablecoins first and agent volume second.

Supply has outrun demand by a wide margin: six protocols for a few thousand dollars a month.

Who sets the limits, and where a person approves

The only approval a stranger can check

Payments solved the problem the rest of this guide keeps running into, and the reason is structural. When an agent pays a merchant it has never dealt with, the merchant needs to know that a real person authorized this purchase, within these limits, and that the authorization will hold up in a dispute. Card networks already had liability rules for chargebacks, so money forced the question "who authorized this?" before anything else did.

Two specifications answer it. AP2, which Google contributed whole to the FIDO Alliance in April 2026, describes intent, cart, and payment mandates, and has more than 60 supporting organizations (AP2 specification). Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, contributed alongside it, makes the chain explicit in three signed layers. An issuer binds the person to a key. The person signs a mandate with constraints. The agent signs the final action. "Each layer cryptographically constrains the next," so a merchant with no prior relationship to the person can verify the whole chain (Verifiable Intent v0.1).

The difference from ordinary sign-in is who signs. In OAuth an authorization server signs on the user's behalf, which proves a policy was checked, not that the person agreed. In a mandate, the person's own device-bound key signs, and a bank vouches for that key. That is why AP2 describes mandates as the evidence a dispute adjudicator uses to decide "whether the user approved the final cart."

What's missing

Early revocation, and everything that isn't money

Mandates have a gap of their own. Verifiable Intent's autonomous mandates, the kind an agent holds while the person is away, last from 24 hours to 30 days, and no reviewed specification can withdraw one before it expires. Every other standard relies on short lifetimes instead, which works only for grants re-issued every few minutes.

The larger gap is scope. Nothing outside payments records, in a form a stranger can verify, what a named person approved: a contract term, a data release, a change to a shipment. The structure exists, but the issuer role that a bank plays in payments has no counterpart in a company's identity provider. FIDO formed an Agentic Authentication working group in April 2026, chaired by CVS Health, Google, and OpenAI, to cover delegated "actions" in general (FIDO Alliance). It is the most likely place for payments' consent model to grow beyond payments.
