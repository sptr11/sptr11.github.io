Background · precedents

History

Every earlier messaging platform shipped an open transport that assumed honest senders, then bolted trust on years later. Agents are repeating the sequence in about two years instead of forty.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The pattern holds across four platforms. The transport is open and assumes nobody lies. Trust arrives later, and usually the large receivers impose it rather than the standards bodies. The value goes to whoever brokers trust between strangers. The agent stack is following the same order, with one difference: the receivers are moving before the standards are finished.

Email: the receivers moved first

SMTP assumed honest senders, and for 24 to 33 years nothing checked them. SPF arrived in 2006 (RFC 4408), DKIM in 2007 (RFC 4871), and DMARC in 2015 (RFC 7489). DMARC then sat as an informational document for eleven years. It reached the standards track as RFC 9989 only in May 2026, more than two years after Gmail began requiring a DMARC record from anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day (Google). Google's bar was low. A policy of p=none, which enforces nothing, is acceptable.

The bolt-on stayed uneven. In 2022, fifteen years after the first DKIM standard, only 28.1% of the top million domains had DKIM enabled, and 2.9% of those were misconfigured (Wang et al., USENIX Security 2022). Most mail providers could detect spoofing "but still allow forged emails to reach the user inbox," and only 9 of 35 showed any visual warning (Hu and Wang, USENIX Security 2018). Administrators held back because deploying brought little benefit unless everyone else deployed too (Hu et al., SecDev 2018).

Spam showed who governs an open transport. Infiltrating the Storm botnet revealed that tiny response rates pay when sending costs nearly nothing (Kanich et al., CCS 2008). Tracing spam's value chain found its chokepoint in a handful of banks that processed the payments (Levchenko et al., IEEE S&P 2011). Filtering by large providers became the main defense (Rao and Reiley, JEP 2012). Agents make persuasive, personalized messages cheaper still, which makes the receiver's filter worth more.

The agent parallel is direct. Cloudflare, Akamai, and AWS verify Web Bot Auth signatures in production, and the IETF's core document has been a working-group draft only since September 2026.

Chat: federation lost to incentives

Open chat federation lost twice, and not for technical reasons. Google ended server-to-server XMPP federation in May 2013 (EFF). Facebook retired its XMPP chat API in 2015, which was a client interface rather than federation. Economics predicted both. Large networks prefer incompatibility (Katz and Shapiro, AER 1985), and standards stall from "excess inertia" until something starts a bandwagon (Farrell and Saloner, RAND 1985).

Interoperability came back only by law. Under the EU's Digital Markets Act, WhatsApp opened third-party chats in Europe in November 2025, with two small partners, opt-in, and no group chats (Meta). Microsoft researchers frame the agent future the same way, as "agentic walled gardens" against an open "web of agents" (Rothschild et al., 2025). For agent transport the forcing function already exists: one neutral foundation hosts both MCP and A2A. The open question has moved up to registries.

The web: the edge captured the value

robots.txt was a voluntary convention in 1994 and a standard only in 2022 (RFC 9309). Measurement shows it does little, while blocking at the network edge works (Liu et al., IMC 2025). The edge networks won because they own delivery and verification together. That complicates a clean story of trust beating transport: on the web, the winner bundled the two. The same companies now verify agent signatures, and from September 2026 Cloudflare blocks assistant fetchers by default on new domains that show ads (see Reading a page for someone).

EDI: the hub mandated it before it was a standard

Walmart announced Internet EDI in September 2002 and required suppliers to use AS2 by 30 September 2003. Many were still struggling "well into 2004" (Food Processing). AS2 itself became RFC 4130 only in July 2005. The hub forced a draft into practice, and the standard followed.

Research on small firms explains the mechanism and adds a warning. Pressure from trading partners drove most EDI adoption, often by firms that gained little from integrating (Iacovou, Benbasat, and Dexter, MIS Quarterly 1995). A powerful buyer can force adoption, but deep use depends on trust between the partners (Hart and Saunders, Organization Science 1997). The intermediaries survived but stayed small. One vendor put the value-added network market near $1B in 2011 (OpenText, vendor estimate), and EDI software today is about a $2.3B market (market-research estimate), small next to email security or content delivery.

The agent languages of the 1990s

Agents have talked to agents before. The multi-agent research of 1980 to 2007 worked out most of the vocabulary today's protocols are rediscovering. KQML, from DARPA's Knowledge Sharing Effort, defined typed messages and routing (Finin et al., CIKM 1994). FIPA, founded in 1996, standardized an agent communication language and dissolved in 2005 into an IEEE committee. Neither language reached wide use.

Munindar Singh's 1998 critique is the hinge. If a message's meaning depends on the sender's private beliefs, nobody can verify that the sender complied, so meaning should be social and publicly observable (Singh, IEEE Computer 1998). Language-model agents sharpen the point. Their "intent" is a distribution shaped by the prompt, which the counterparty can't inspect and the counterparty's own message can rewrite. That is why indirect prompt injection matters so much between companies (Greshake et al., AISec 2023). A message from another organization is, by definition, content from outside the trust boundary.

The classic literature failed commercially for four reasons. A leader of the field conceded in 2007 that no real agent ecosystem existed (Hendler, IEEE Intelligent Systems). Agents lacked general competence, so they had little to say to each other. Every domain needed a hand-built ontology. And there was no trust layer between organizations. Language models remove the first two: in Agora, agents negotiate structured protocols in natural language and fall back to prose for rare exchanges (Marro et al., 2024). Neutral foundations address the third. Trust between strangers is the one still open.

Six lessons

1. Transport goes open, and trust ends up concentrated

SMTP and HTTP are open, but a handful of receivers decide what gets through. On the web the winners bundled verification into delivery. Expect a few verifiers to decide which agents are real, and expect them to be the companies already in the delivery path.

2. Authentication lands on the domain, not the person

DMARC proves a message came from example.com, not from a particular person who works there. Agent Cards, .well-known manifests, and Web Bot Auth key directories all sit at the domain too. Delegation to a named person is the unsolved part.

3. Federation needs a forcing function

It took regulation or a carrier standard to bring chat interoperability back. For agent transport the forcing function is a single neutral foundation. The fight has moved up to registries.

4. Mandates get adoption, and trust gets usage

EDI spread because Walmart required it. The first cross-company agent protocols will likely spread the same way, inside one large buyer's supply base. A mandate buys compliance, not depth, so the supplier has to get something out of it too.

5. Intermediaries survive, but stay small

The value-added networks outlived predictions of their death and never grew large. A neutral agent intermediary earns its keep on the same three jobs, onboarding, mapping, and audit, and needs a second source of value to grow.

6. Semantics was the old blocker, and persuasion is the new one

KQML and FIPA stalled because every pair of agents needed a shared ontology. Language-model agents agree on meaning in plain language, which is why adoption is fast this time. But a receiver that understands anything can also be talked into anything. The 1990s problem was agents that couldn't understand each other. Today's problem is agents that understand each other too well.
