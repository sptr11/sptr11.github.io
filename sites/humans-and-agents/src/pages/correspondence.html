Use case 5 · ↔ both sides drafting

Correspondence between companies

Most work between two companies still moves by email. Agents now write much of it, people still press Send, and nobody measures how much of that business mail is written by software.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The short answer

A person and an agent on one side write to a person and an agent on the other. The message travels by email or a link, because those reach anyone with no setup. Each sender decides what goes out, and the Send click is the only approval. What's missing is proof of which company sent a message, and an approval step the drafting agent can't rewrite.

Who is on each end

Two drafters and two people who press Send

This cell is plausibly the largest of the 12, and it is unmeasured. No study measures what share of legitimate business email is AI-drafted. The closest measurement covers malicious mail. A peer-reviewed study of 481,558 filtered emails found that at least 51% of spam and about 14% of business email compromise was written by a language model by April 2025 (Hao et al., IMC 2025). Business email compromise is targeted, business-style prose, so its share is the best available proxy for ordinary professional mail.

Agents are getting addresses of their own. AgentMail, which issues email inboxes to agents, raised a $6M seed round in March 2026 and reports more than 500 business customers (The Next Web). One of the plainest routes from an agent to the outside world is a protocol from 1982.

What carries the message

Email, because it needs nobody's permission

Email reaches any counterparty with no arrangement between the two companies, at about 362B messages a day across business and consumer use (Radicati, 2024). Shared channels need both sides to opt in. Slack Connect reached 77 of the Fortune 100 by 2022 (Slack). Teams shared channels need both tenants to configure B2B direct connect, and restrict third-party apps to allowlisted tabs by default (Microsoft Learn).

That asymmetry decides where agents go. An agent posting into a shared channel inherits the platform's two-sided approval. An agent sending email does not. The trust in a shared channel is also thinner than it looks. Slack's verified-organization badge works at the level of the organization, and Slack says it "cannot fully guarantee that an organization's identity is legitimate" (Slack Help). In 2023 its engineers wrote that Slack's policy controls "are not connection aware" (Slack Engineering).

The agent protocols have barely reached this cell. Discoverable A2A agents number in the hundreds, against roughly 19,000 entries in the MCP registry, which connects agents to tools rather than to other companies (MCP Queen, secondary). A2A lets an agent sign its Agent Card but doesn't require it. The first crawl of those cards found seven on 10 hosts, six valid and none signed (hvtracker).

Who sets the limits, and where a person approves

The Send button is the whole control

In this mode each side's person approves what leaves, one message at a time. Security research has converged on this design. Simon Willison calls the dangerous combination the "lethal trifecta": an agent that reads untrusted input, can reach private data, and has a way to send (Willison). Meta's "Agents Rule of Two" says an agent may do any two of those three in a session, but not all three without supervision (Willison's summary). An agent that reads the other company's mail and drafts a reply, with a person sending it, follows that rule.

The attacks that matter already cross company lines this way, because an outsider's content reaches an insider's agent. EchoLeak used one external email to pull privileged data out of Microsoft 365 Copilot (CVE-2025-32711). ForcedLeak planted its payload in a Salesforce web-to-lead form and exfiltrated through an allowlisted domain that had expired and could be bought for about $5 (The Hacker News). The first malicious MCP server quietly blind-copied every email it sent to an attacker, after 1,643 downloads (Koi).

The weak point of human approval is the human. People approve 93% of permission prompts.

Anthropic reports that 93% figure for manual permission prompts in Claude Code (Anthropic). In one MCP attack, whitespace pushed stolen data off the edge of the approval screen (Willison). Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 concludes that approval must run through a channel "the LLM cannot influence" (Unit 42). An approval screen written by the drafting agent fails that test.

What's missing

A provable sender, and an approval the agent can't write

Email solved half of this decades ago. DKIM lets a receiver check which domain sent a message. A message delivered by link has no equivalent: it proves which host served the page, not which company wrote it. The pieces for a fix exist. A2A Agent Cards can be signed, and Web Bot Auth publishes keys at a well-known path on the sender's domain. Nothing yet applies them to a message a person sends.

The other gap is the approval itself. An approval step that resists manipulation would show the raw outbound fields rather than an agent's summary, make unusual recipients and attachments stand out, and draw its text from somewhere the agent can't reach. OpenAI says prompt injection "is unlikely to ever be fully 'solved'" and compares it to scams (Fortune). If the comparison holds, the defense will look like email's: authenticated senders, filtering at the receiver, and a person who can see what they are approving.
