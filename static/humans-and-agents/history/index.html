Background · precedents

History

Email, EDI, chat, and the web each started with an open transport and added trust years later. Agents are going through the same steps in about two years.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

Every earlier way of sending messages between organizations went through the same three steps. The transport came first, and it assumed nobody would lie. Trust came years later, usually imposed by the biggest receivers rather than by a standards body. And the money went to whoever stood between strangers and vouched for them.

Agents are going through the same steps, much faster. The difference this time is that the receivers are acting before the standards are finished.

EDI: the first time companies wired their machines together

The closest precedent for agents between companies is EDI, the standard way businesses have exchanged purchase orders, invoices, and shipping notices since the 1980s. For years it ran over private value-added networks that charged by volume. In September 2002 Walmart told its suppliers to switch to EDI over the internet using AS2, with a deadline of 30 September 2003. Many were still struggling "well into 2004" (Food Processing). AS2 didn't become a standard, RFC 4130, until July 2005. The buyer forced a draft into practice, and the standard caught up later.

Research on small firms explains why it worked and where it fell short. Most adopted EDI because a big trading partner required it, and many gained little from it (Iacovou, Benbasat, and Dexter, 1995). A powerful buyer can force adoption, but deep use depends on trust between the partners (Hart and Saunders, 1997). The networks in the middle survived by handling onboarding, document mapping, and audit trails, but they stayed small. One vendor put that market near $1B in 2011 (OpenText, vendor estimate), and EDI software today is about a $2.3B market (market-research estimate).

The same pattern is showing up with agents. Walmart's negotiation agent and the freight tendering hubs are large buyers setting the terms for their suppliers.

Email: the receivers set the rules

SMTP, from 1982, let any server claim to send mail for any domain. The checks came 24 to 33 years later: SPF in 2006 (RFC 4408), DKIM in 2007 (RFC 4871), and DMARC in 2015 (RFC 7489). DMARC then stayed an informational document for 11 years. It became a full standard, RFC 9989, only in May 2026, more than two years after Gmail started requiring a DMARC record from anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day (Google). Even then Gmail accepted p=none, a policy that enforces nothing.

Adoption stayed patchy. In 2022, 15 years after the first DKIM standard, only 28.1% of the top million domains had DKIM turned on, and 2.9% of those had it misconfigured (Wang et al., 2022). Most mail providers could detect spoofed mail but still delivered it to the inbox, and only 9 of 35 showed a warning (Hu and Wang, 2018). Administrators held off because deploying did little until everyone else did (Hu et al., 2018).

Spam showed who really governs an open network. Studies of the Storm botnet found that tiny response rates pay off when sending costs almost nothing (Kanich et al., 2008). Tracing spam's money led to a handful of banks processing its payments (Levchenko et al., 2011). Filtering by the big providers became the main defense (Rao and Reiley, 2012). Agents make convincing, personalized messages even cheaper to send, so the receiving side's filter matters more.

The same thing is happening with agents. Cloudflare, Akamai, and AWS already verify Web Bot Auth signatures in production, and the IETF only adopted the core draft as a working-group document in September 2026.

Chat and the web

Open chat federation lost twice, and not for technical reasons. Google ended XMPP federation in May 2013 (EFF). Large networks prefer not to interoperate (Katz and Shapiro, 1985), and standards stall until something starts a bandwagon (Farrell and Saloner, 1985). Interoperability came back only by law, when the EU's Digital Markets Act made WhatsApp open third-party chats in November 2025, with two small partners and no group chats (Meta). Agent transport has avoided that fate so far by sitting in one neutral foundation. The risk has moved up to the registries that say which agents are real.

On the web, robots.txt was a voluntary convention from 1994 that became a standard only in 2022 (RFC 9309). It does little, and blocking at the network edge does a lot (Liu et al., 2025). The edge networks won because they own both delivery and verification. They're now the ones verifying agent signatures, and since September 2026 Cloudflare blocks assistant fetchers by default on new domains that show ads.

Agents talked to agents once before

Multi-agent research from 1980 to 2007 worked out most of what today's protocols are rediscovering. KQML, from DARPA's Knowledge Sharing Effort, defined typed messages and routing (Finin et al., 1994). FIPA, founded in 1996, standardized an agent communication language and folded into an IEEE committee in 2005. Neither spread much beyond research labs and a few telecom systems.

Munindar Singh pointed out the core problem in 1998. If what a message means depends on what the sender privately believes, nobody can check that the sender kept its word, so meaning has to be public and observable (Singh, 1998). Language-model agents make this worse. Their "intent" depends on their prompt, the counterparty can't inspect it, and the counterparty's own message can change it. That's why prompt injection matters so much between companies (Greshake et al., 2023). Every message from another company is content from outside your trust boundary.

The old work failed commercially for four reasons. By 2007 a leading researcher conceded there was no real agent ecosystem (Hendler, 2007). The agents weren't capable enough to have much to say. Every industry needed a hand-built vocabulary. And there was no trust layer between companies. Language models fixed the first two: in Agora, agents agree on structured protocols in plain language and fall back to prose for unusual cases (Marro et al., 2024). Neutral foundations are fixing the third. Trust between companies is the one still open.

Six lessons

1. Transport goes open, and trust ends up concentrated

SMTP and HTTP are open, but a handful of receivers decide what gets through. On the web the winners bundled verification into delivery. Expect a few verifiers to decide which agents are real, and expect them to be the companies already in the delivery path.

2. Authentication lands on the domain, not the person

DMARC proves a message came from example.com, not from a particular person who works there. Agent Cards, .well-known manifests, and Web Bot Auth key directories all sit at the domain too. Delegation to a named person is the unsolved part.

3. Federation needs a forcing function

It took regulation or a carrier standard to bring chat interoperability back. For agent transport the forcing function is a single neutral foundation. The fight has moved up to registries.

4. Mandates get adoption, and trust gets usage

EDI spread because Walmart required it. The first cross-company agent protocols will likely spread the same way, inside one large buyer's supply base. A mandate buys compliance, not depth, so the supplier has to get something out of it too.

5. Intermediaries survive, but stay small

The value-added networks outlived predictions of their death and never grew large. A neutral agent intermediary earns its keep on the same three jobs, onboarding, mapping, and audit, and needs a second source of value to grow.

6. Semantics was the old blocker, and persuasion is the new one

KQML and FIPA stalled because every pair of agents needed a shared ontology. Language-model agents agree on meaning in plain language, which is why adoption is fast this time. But a receiver that understands anything can also be talked into anything. The 1990s problem was agents that couldn't understand each other. Today's problem is agents that understand each other too well.
