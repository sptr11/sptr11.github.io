Approving and paying

When an agent commits a company to something, the other side needs to know a real person approved it, within limits, and that the approval will hold up in a dispute. Payments have worked out how to prove that. Nothing else has.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

Who's involved

A person who can commit their company, the agent acting for them, and a counterparty who has to accept the commitment. In business that commitment might be a payment, a purchase order, a change to contract terms, a credit on an account, or approval to release data. Only the first has agent-era standards.

The busiest agent payments between organizations are machines paying machines. Under x402, a server answers a request with HTTP status 402, Payment Required, names a price, and the agent pays it in a stablecoin. Most of it is developer tools paying per API call from wallets that people fund. On the Base network x402 has carried more than 138M transactions, 87% of them under 10 cents (Chainalysis via AI Weekly).

The money involved is small. TRM Labs counts about $25.6M of likely commerce over x402 since May 2025, of which only 0.6% to 7.5% looks agentic, and puts actual agent payment activity in 2026 at roughly $5,000 to $11,000 a month (TRM Labs). At least six payment protocols are competing for it, and only x402 publishes any volume.

How the approval travels

The card networks built the model the rest of business will probably borrow. Their agent programs are aimed at consumer shopping for now. Visa Intelligent Commerce, live in Europe with more than 30 issuers, has the cardholder confirm each purchase with a passkey (Visa). Mastercard and Santander ran a live payment where the account holder set caps and merchant categories ahead of time and approved nothing at the moment of sale (ai2.work).

Underneath both is the mandate. Google's AP2, now at the FIDO Alliance, describes intent, cart, and payment mandates (AP2). Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, contributed to FIDO alongside it, spells out the chain in three signed layers. A bank binds the person to a key. The person signs a mandate with limits. The agent signs the final action. "Each layer cryptographically constrains the next," so a merchant that has never dealt with the person can check the whole chain (Verifiable Intent v0.1).

Who approves

The person, with their own key. That's the difference from how sign-in works at the office. With OAuth, the company's authorization server signs on the employee's behalf. That proves a policy was checked, not that the employee agreed. A mandate is signed by the person's own device-bound key, and a bank vouches for that key. AP2 describes mandates as the evidence a dispute adjudicator uses to decide "whether the user approved the final cart."

Payments got here first because card networks already had chargebacks and liability rules. When money moves between strangers, someone has to be able to answer "who authorized this?" A purchase order or a contract change raises the same question, and today the answer is an email thread.

What's missing

A mandate for everything that isn't a payment. Nothing records, in a form another company can verify, that a named person approved a contract term, a data release, a credit, or a change to an order. The three-layer structure would carry over directly. What's missing is the issuer: the role a bank plays in payments has no counterpart in a company's identity provider.

A way to withdraw an approval early. Verifiable Intent's autonomous mandates, the kind an agent holds while its person is away, last from 24 hours to 30 days, and no reviewed specification can revoke one before it expires. Everything else relies on short-lived tokens, which only works for grants re-issued every few minutes.

The most likely place for this to get built is FIDO's Agentic Authentication working group, formed in April 2026 and chaired by CVS Health, Google, and OpenAI. Its charter covers delegating "actions," not just payments (FIDO Alliance).
