Background · the stack

Protocols

Every layer of the agent stack now has a serious contender except the top one: a record, between organizations, of what a person approved. That layer exists only for payments.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026 · statuses checked against specification text, the IETF Datatracker, and the RFC Editor

The stack is being built from the bottom up, the way email's was. Transport is settled enough to build on, and it is neutral. MCP, A2A, AGENTS.md, goose, and agentgateway all sit in the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, whose platinum members include AWS, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI (Linux Foundation). x402 has its own Linux Foundation home. AP2 and Verifiable Intent sit at the FIDO Alliance.

That arrangement largely removes the risk that agent networks close the way consumer chat did. The competition has moved up a layer, to identity, registries, and verification.

How the standards handle delegation and consent

A cross-company agent exchange needs six things to be checkable: which organization sent it, which agent, on behalf of which named person, within what scope, whether the authority can be withdrawn, and whether a record survives. The scorecard rates 13 standards on those six properties, from the specification text rather than vendor descriptions.

F means a third party can verify the property cryptographically. P means it holds only inside one domain, depends on an optional field, or proves who sent a request without proving what was authorized. N means it is out of scope.

Transport standards identify a domain and leave the person to someone else

A2A says identity "is handled at the protocol layer, not within A2A semantics" (A2A specification). Web Bot Auth signatures "do not express authorization, delegation, or consent." ANP's identity documents have no field for a human's authorization. The OAuth family does carry a named person, but only through an identity provider the other side already trusts, or inside one domain. ID-JAG requires the receiving side to "independently trust" the same identity provider, and makes the agent itself an optional claim (ID-JAG -04).

Only the payment mandates are signed by the person

In OAuth, an authorization server signs on the user's behalf, which proves that policy was evaluated, not that the person agreed. In Verifiable Intent, an issuer binds the person to a key, the person signs a mandate with constraints, and the agent signs the final action. A merchant with no prior relationship to the person can check every link. Card payments are the one place delegation between strangers works, because the issuing bank supplies a trust root both sides already accept.

Nothing can be withdrawn early

No reviewed standard defines a revocation list or endpoint for agent delegations. Everything relies on short lifetimes: about five minutes for an ID-JAG grant, 60 seconds in Identity Chaining's examples, 24 hours or less for a Web Bot Auth signature. Web Bot Auth says outright that removing a key "is not a revocation mechanism." Short lifetimes work for grants that are re-issued every few minutes. They fail for the delegations a cross-company agent would actually hold, such as Verifiable Intent's autonomous mandates, valid for up to 30 days.

Company A's agent, acting for a person, at Company B

On paper, two drafts can express it: ID-JAG and OAuth Identity Chaining. Both let A's identity provider issue a signed grant naming the person, which B's authorization server redeems. Both also require B to trust A's identity provider in advance and to know the person already. Identity Chaining tells B to "deny the request if it cannot identify the subject" (Identity Chaining -17).

In practice, Okta's Cross App Access works mostly inside one customer: the software vendor can resolve the person because the person is already a provisioned user of that customer's account at the vendor. That is A's data hosted by B, not B's agent serving a stranger. Microsoft says Entra agent identities "can't access resources or APIs in other tenants." NIST says managing external agents from untrusted sources "will not be addressed under this initial effort." The only agent-specific on-behalf-of draft at the IETF expired in February 2026 without adoption (draft-02).

No shipped product lets one company's identity provider vouch for a person to another company's agent when that person has no account there. The Personal Agent Protocol routes around the problem for consumers, because the person signs in on the company's own page. Between two companies, that route doesn't exist.
