Background · open problems

What's missing

For agents working across companies, the open questions are who sets the limits and who can prove what was approved.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

Every working autonomous exchange runs inside limits that one party set alone. Every working identity standard binds a person only inside a boundary that already exists. The one domain that has solved delegation between strangers, card payments, did it by adding a third-party issuer and a record the person signs. What's missing elsewhere is narrower than it first appears.

Six gaps

Each gap is absent from the specifications and products that have shipped. Wider adoption of what exists would not close any of them.

Enforcement has to be deterministic

A gateway that asks a language model to judge whether a message is safe will behave like the detectors that adaptive attacks have already broken. One study broke all 12 published defenses it tested, most at more than 90% success; one product went from 0% to 90%, and another from 5% to 96% (Nasr et al., 2025). Isolation by design holds up better. CaMeL completes 77% of tasks on the AgentDojo benchmark with provable security, against 84% with no defense at all (Debenedetti et al., 2025).

The design-patterns literature states the principle plainly. Once an agent reads untrusted input, it "must be constrained so that it is impossible for that input to trigger any consequential actions" (Beurer-Kellner et al., 2025). For a consent layer between companies, that means rules that don't read the prose: field-level limits on what may leave, destination allowlists that expire, caps on value and rate, and one counterparty per session. ForcedLeak got through an allowlisted domain that had lapsed and cost about $5 to buy back.

Adoption through hubs, usage through trust

A hub buyer can require a consent layer of its suppliers, as Walmart required AS2. Freight's tender corridors and large retailers' supplier channels are the obvious candidates. A mandate buys compliance, not depth, so the record has to be worth something to the supplier as well. Payment mandates show how: they serve the merchant as evidence in a chargeback.

Who might close the gaps

The bodies most likely to fill the first three gaps have only just convened. FIDO's Agentic Authentication working group formed in April 2026 to cover delegated actions generally. The IETF's WIMSE group adopted its AIMS profile for agent authentication in September 2026. The W3C's Agent Identity Registry community group, proposed in April 2026, plans a protocol for negotiating trust between organizations. The OpenID Foundation's AI identity group says current frameworks don't handle recursive delegation or trust across domains (OpenID Foundation).

Trust is arriving out of order

Email got its trust layer last, decades after the transport. Agents are getting pieces of theirs early and in the wrong sequence. The edges enforce verification and price agent reads before the IETF has finished the signature standard. Acquirers pay billions for agent identity before any vendor reports agent revenue. The security literature settled on isolation and human approval before cross-company agent traffic existed in any volume.

Two consequences follow. Anything that depends on assistants reading a link will have its reachability decided by edge defaults it doesn't control. And the consent layer between companies may be claimed by acquisition or standardization before it is claimed by use. The specification work at FIDO and the IETF over the next two years is the thing to watch.
