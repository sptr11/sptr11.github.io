Use case 1 · → agent to person, across companies

Buying and negotiating

Procurement is the most mature cross-company agent work. A buyer's agent bargains with a supplier's person, inside limits only the buyer set.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

The short answer

A buyer's agent writes and a supplier's person answers. The messages travel by chat, email, phone, and load board rather than agent protocols. The buyer sets every limit in advance, the supplier's person accepts or declines, and neither side's agent can see the other's limits.

Who is on each end

A bot on one side, a sales rep on the other

Walmart was early. Its negotiation agent, built by Pactum, bargains over goods Walmart uses rather than goods it sells, and over smaller contracts, the long tail no buyer has time for (PYMNTS, citing Bloomberg). It can run up to 2,000 negotiations at once, and it closed deals with 68% of the suppliers it approached (HBR, 2022). Pactum now reports $55B of spend under management across Walmart, Honeywell, and more than 50 other enterprises (company figure).

The person on the other side seems not to mind. In 2023, 75% of Walmart's suppliers said they preferred the bot to a human buyer (Engadget, citing Bloomberg). Pactum now puts the figure at 82%.

Freight runs the same play faster. project44's Freight Procurement Agent, generally available since March 2026, has sent about a million messages to carriers and cut shippers' spend by 4.1% (company figures). Chain's Autopilot Booking Agent, launched in June 2026, negotiates loads with carriers over load boards and email (Chain). HappyRobot's voice agents, sold to more than 150 enterprise customers, phone carriers directly (FreightWaves).

Most carriers are small, and they answer by hand. Parade's customer data shows about three inbound emails for every phone call at a brokerage, with 80% of that contact coming from carriers that run fewer than 25 trucks (vendor data, no sample disclosed). That long tail will never stand up an agent endpoint.

Some carriers do have agents, though. Hey Bubba and Fleetworks sell carrier-side agents that work the same inboxes, phone lines, and load boards as the brokers' agents. Some freight exchanges are therefore probably agent to agent over human channels, with neither side aware of it. No source documents this yet.

What carries the message

Human channels, not agent protocols

The buyer's agent reaches the supplier where the supplier already works: a chat window, an inbox, a phone, a load board. A2A, the agent-to-agent protocol, has more than 150 supporting organizations and no named deployment between two companies (Linux Foundation, Apr 2026).

Two public cases put language-model agents on both sides. Tyson Foods and Gordon Food Service piloted A2A to exchange product data and sales leads, which commits neither company to anything (Google Cloud). In 2023 Luminance had two instances of its software negotiate a nondisclosure agreement, with lawyers watching and a person signing (CNBC).

At the far end of automation sits the hub. C.H. Robinson accepts tenders automatically for more than 5,200 customers in under 90 seconds, against up to four hours for a person (company figure). That is fast, but it is rules on both sides, not two reasoning agents bargaining. Its ancestor is the Contract Net protocol of 1980, which already had tasks announced, bids submitted, and awards made (Smith, IEEE Transactions on Computers). The bids now come from people, in plain language.

Who sets the limits, and where a person approves

The buyer writes the envelope

Every deployment works the same way. The stronger or initiating party fixes the limits before the first message, the agent bargains inside them, and anything outside them goes to a person. Walmart sets the ceiling, and the supplier's representative accepts or declines. project44's agent renews contracts "within approved limits" and routes new carriers and spot awards to human review. Chain's agent takes a start, target, and maximum rate from the broker's transport management system and escalates when a carrier asks for more.

Approval has moved from each message to the envelope as a whole. That makes the agents fast, but only one side of the deal wrote the envelope.

Suppliers accept Walmart's limits. They don't negotiate them.

Read the satisfaction figures with that in mind. The 75% to 82% of suppliers who prefer the bot are content with terms they had no hand in setting. The agents are efficient at executing one party's policy, and nobody has yet tested them as negotiators between equals.

What's missing

A way for both sides to show their limits

Real negotiation between two agents needs a step that none of the 25 public deployments performs: each side disclosing its limits to the other and checking them, machine to machine, before bargaining starts. The multi-agent research of the 1990s worked out concession strategies for exactly this (Faratin, Sierra, and Jennings, 1998; Jennings et al., 2001). In production today the limits are written by one side and never exchanged.

The early experiments show why that step matters. In Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace, every model tested took the first acceptable offer 60% to 100% of the time, which hands an advantage to whichever counterparty answers first (Bansal et al., 2025). In agent-against-agent bargaining, gaps in model capability produce lopsided outcomes, overpayment, and broken constraints (Zhu et al., 2025). Pricing agents built on language models drift to supracompetitive prices without being told to (Fish et al., ACM EC 2026).

Buyers want to go further anyway. In one vendor-sponsored survey, 53% of executives expected agents to negotiate with customers or suppliers within 12 months, and 56% were very concerned about agents acting without guardrails (Icertis). In supply chains, only about a fifth support fully automated procurement (Economist Impact).

The party that writes the envelope today is also the one best placed to require a standard for it. Walmart did exactly that with Internet EDI, announced in September 2002 with a one-year deadline for suppliers (see History).
