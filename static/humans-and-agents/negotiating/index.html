Across companies · 6

Negotiating and buying

Procurement is where agents work across company lines at real volume. A buyer's agent bargains with a supplier's person, inside limits the buyer set before the conversation started.

Evidence current to 18 October 2026

Who's involved

Walmart was early. Its negotiation agent, built by Pactum, bargains with suppliers over the goods Walmart uses rather than the goods it sells, the long tail of smaller contracts no buyer has time for. It can run up to 2,000 negotiations at once and closed deals with 68% of the suppliers it approached. Pactum now reports $55B of spend under management across Walmart, Honeywell, and more than 50 other companies.

The supplier's side is usually a sales rep in a chat window. They seem not to mind: in 2023, 75% of Walmart's suppliers said they preferred the bot to a human buyer, and Pactum now puts the figure at 82%.

Freight runs the same play faster. project44's Freight Procurement Agent, generally available since March 2026, has sent about a million messages to carriers and cut shippers' spend by 4.1%, by the company's count. Chain's Autopilot Booking Agent, launched in June 2026, negotiates loads with carriers over load boards and email. HappyRobot's voice agents, sold to more than 150 enterprise customers, phone carriers directly.

Most carriers are small and answer by hand. Parade's data shows about three inbound emails for every phone call at a brokerage, with 80% of that contact coming from carriers that run fewer than 25 trucks. That long tail will never run an agent endpoint of its own.

How the messages travel

Through the channels the supplier already uses: chat, email, phone, and load boards. None of these deployments uses an agent-to-agent protocol, because the other side is a person.

At the far end of automation sits the hub. C.H. Robinson accepts tenders automatically for more than 5,200 customers in under 90 seconds, where a person might take up to four hours. That's fast, and it runs on fixed rules at both ends. Its ancestor is the Contract Net protocol from 1980, which already had tasks announced, bids submitted, and awards made. The bids now come from people, in plain language.

Who sets the limits

The buyer, every time. Walmart sets the ceiling, and the supplier's rep accepts or declines. project44's agent renews contracts "within approved limits" and sends new carriers and spot awards to a person for review. Chain's agent takes a start, target, and maximum rate from the broker's transport management system and escalates when a carrier asks for more.

So approval has moved from each message to the envelope as a whole. That makes the agents fast. It also means only one side of the deal wrote the rules. Suppliers accept Walmart's limits; they don't negotiate them. The 75% to 82% who prefer the bot are happy with terms they had no hand in setting.

What's missing

A way for both sides to show their limits. Negotiation between two companies' agents would need each side to disclose what it may agree to and check the other's before bargaining starts. None of the 25 public deployments does that. Multi-agent research worked out concession strategies for it in the 1990s, but in production the limits are written by one side and never exchanged.

The early experiments show what goes wrong without that step. In Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace, every model tested took the first acceptable offer 60% to 100% of the time. When agents of different capability negotiate, the outcomes tilt toward the stronger model, with overpayment and broken constraints. Pricing agents drift to prices above the competitive level without being told to.

Buyers want to go further anyway. In a vendor-sponsored survey, 53% of executives expected agents to negotiate with customers or suppliers within 12 months, and 56% were very concerned about agents acting without guardrails. Only about a fifth of supply-chain leaders support fully automated procurement.

The company that writes the limits today is also best placed to require a standard for them. Walmart did that once already, when it told suppliers in September 2002 to move to Internet EDI within a year.
